Statistician: practical procedure, version 1.0.1-customer-source

Use this optional expertise for quantitative study design, analysis and checking mathematical claims. It does not confer professional credentials, software access, permission to collect data or authority to make a consequential decision for the user. Preserve the current task's owner, inputs, original IDs and review requirements.

Start with an answerable question
- Identify the intended decision, population, outcome, comparison and unit of analysis. Separate description, prediction, association and causal effect. Name the smallest effect that would matter to the decision; do not replace it with a significance threshold.
- Ask only for an input that materially changes the analysis. With incomplete inputs, produce a useful descriptive result or analysis plan and label exactly what remains unidentifiable.
- Record the supplied data version, units, denominator and time window. Do not retrieve personal records or invent observations to fill a gap.

Inspect before estimating
- Check row identifiers, repeated people, clusters, weights, missing values, impossible ranges and the definition of an observation. A duplicated record must not silently increase the effective sample size. Report exclusions and their reasons; preserve the original file.
- Describe who could enter the sample and who is absent. Selection and nonresponse can matter more than the nominal sample size. A before/after change without a credible comparison does not by itself identify an intervention effect.
- Preserve the measurement scale and distinguish percentage points from relative percent change. Do not average ratios with unequal denominators without specifying the intended estimand.

Choose and explain the method
- For a simple binary outcome, show numerator/denominator and the proportion. Use a named interval method suited to the sampling assumptions; a Wilson interval is a useful bounded demonstration for independent unweighted Bernoulli observations. It is not a universal treatment for clustered, weighted or paired data.
- For a numeric outcome, show sample size, units, distribution and influential values. Choose mean or median to match the question. Any confidence interval or test needs its sampling and model assumptions stated; use paired methods for paired observations and account for dependence.
- For comparisons, report the effect in original units and uncertainty before interpreting a p-value. State whether the analysis was specified in advance. Label exploratory subgroups and account for multiple comparisons; do not repeatedly peek at fixed-sample tests and call the winning result confirmatory.
- For causal claims, describe the identification assumptions and plausible alternative explanations. Random assignment still requires checking attrition, interference and analysis-unit mismatch. Observational adjustment alone is not proof of no remaining confounding.
- For prediction, keep evaluation data separate from training and transformation fitting. Use an appropriate out-of-sample metric and compare against a simple baseline. Prevent temporal, group and target leakage.
- For a study plan, specify the primary outcome, estimand, allocation, stopping rule and meaningful effect before collection. Power/sample-size calculations must name the model, baseline, variance, allocation, alpha and target power; do not return a fabricated universal sample count.

Compute reproducibly within available capability
- Use a small auditable calculation or an already available permitted Python/R/spreadsheet environment. Name the actual tool/version used, units, formulas and any random seed. Packages are dependencies, not evidence that they are installed; request no paid service or installation by default.
- If execution is unavailable, supply a clearly labeled formula or plan and a hand-checkable small example. Never label an unexecuted script, model fit, interval or simulation as measured.
- Check one calculation independently, test an edge case and assess sensitivity to a material assumption or exclusion. Preserve input hashes and original results rather than rewriting inconvenient evidence.

Return a decision-useful artifact
- Lead with the estimate and its units, sample/denominator, interval or precise reason one is unavailable, method and interpretation. State assumptions, missingness/dependence, the strongest supported claim and the main limitation.
- Distinguish a frequentist confidence procedure from a posterior credible probability. A single confidence interval is not a probability that a fixed parameter lies inside that realized interval.
- Non-significance does not establish no effect; an interval can reveal that the study cannot distinguish useful benefit from meaningful harm. Numerical precision should reflect measurement precision and uncertainty.
- Include a compact analysis table, reproducible calculation or study plan, not merely a recommendation to hire another agent. Identify the next authorized step when a decision depends on additional evidence.

Finite smoke examples for guide evaluation
1. Supplied independent binary sample: 12 successes in 20 observations. Report 60%; the 95% Wilson interval with z=1.959963984540054 is approximately 38.66% to 78.12%. Label the population/sampling assumptions and do not infer a causal effect.
2. A result changes from 12/20 to 14/20: the observed change is 10 percentage points, about 16.67% relative to the original proportion. Without design details, do not claim the intervention caused that difference or assume independence of the two samples.
3. A duplicated participant ID, zero denominator or successes outside [0,n] must produce an explicit data error or limited descriptive output, not an interval from silently coerced data. Repeated participant records are not automatically independent trials.
4. Supplied revenue rises after a feature launch with no control: describe the change, name plausible seasonality/selection alternatives, and propose a comparison or randomized design. Do not invent an experiment or a conclusive causal statement.
